# The full playthrough: the pre-release verification component (method revision 2026-10-24)

The third verification workflow of METHOD.md, written as a reusable component. It is **pre-release testing that mimics a human tester**: automated, internal, nobody but the owner reads its output. It is not a player feature and must not be wired into any player-facing layer.

## 1. What it is
- A **plan** (`acceptance/<title>/play/playthrough.json` + `PLAYTHROUGH.md`) derived from the decoded game: the main line from title screen to finale, every step citing its decode site (script address, table, record offset). Its shape is the loop a human plays: a sequence of **campaign fights**, each preceded by the side quests and content that stage the party for it (the guides say which, and to what level they bring the party), then the fight, then the next campaign fight. A chapter of the plan is one campaign fight with its staging content, in that order.
- An **executor**: the title's scripted autoplay bot on the virtual clock, reading the plan, with no model in the loop at run time.
- A **blocker loop**, run in **batch mode**: the executor does not stop at the first blocker; it logs it with its bundle, applies the safest skip (defer the step, engine default, reload and skip the block) and plays on to the end of the chapter, so one replay collects every blocker and the specialists fix them in parallel; one more replay confirms. Chapters with checkpoints replay in parallel slices. A missing executor capability still stops the slice, listing every one found. The serial form, one fix per blocker per replay, costs ten to thirty minutes a blocker and is what made the Gold Box pilot slow. Per blocker the bundle holds the state save, event ring, screenshot, the failing step and its cite; the Foreman triages it as a PLAN bug (the planner amends), a PORT bug (a fix lane, the cross-title bug rule) or an ENGINE bug; the run resumes from the last checkpoint. Guards: a time-box per iteration, a cap on iterations per title, and an escalation chain when a lane thrashes (a second attempt at the same blocker, or one open past the time-box): the lane consults the Strategy Expert with the concrete question and the bundle; if that answer still fails, the Strategy Expert **researches the specific stuck event online** the way an expert human does when stuck (targeted queries in the player's own words for the map, event, monster or character; FAQs and message boards, game wikis, store community forums, archived newsgroups and play-through archives), records what it finds in the knowledge file with citations and keeps a ranked list of sources per era and genre, learned from which source actually answered (for 1980s and 1990s computer RPGs: the official clue books, FAQ archives and their boards, the game wikis, store forums, the long-running RPG communities, archived newsgroups and magazine scans; general social media ranks last), and only then is the owner asked, in plain game terms, with what was tried. No blocker gets a third blind attempt.
- **The run is always running.** The executor plays the plan as it stands until a real blocker; the specialists fix forward from blockers; planning happens around a running game, never instead of one. No review gates the run except the static check for the chapter being played (owner, Gold Box pilot: an afternoon of re-planning without a live step).
- The **gate run**: once the plan converges, a deterministic (fixed seed), model-free run, title screen to finale, in minutes of wall time. `FULL PLAYTHROUGH PASS` = 0 failures, every main-line block entered, saves round-trip, the per-title detectors (same-side attacks, journal references, key delivery, ...) clean. Rerun after every cross-title fix. **No release is called complete without it.**

## 1b. How the plan is made: play the walkthrough one step at a time
The plan is not written before play. A **live player** (a model in the loop, on the cheapest capable lane) holds the walkthrough as a step list, from the title screen onward, and plays the running game **one step at a time**: read the step, act, read the screen, next step. Routine movement along a known path and fights with known tactics run on the executor's policies; but **never between clicks**: the model translates the whole numbered walkthrough into executor steps in one pass, the executor plays the entire list at machine speed collecting failures, and the model touches only the failed steps, in which case it consults the Strategy Expert, then researches, then the owner. A game action takes milliseconds; a model deliberating per step turns a two-minute game into a two-day one (the Gold Box pilot). Every decision is **recorded as a trace**; the trace is the plan, and its model-free replay is the speedrun gate. The domain specialists are on-demand helpers the player calls for a fight plan or a stop, not pre-planners. The owner's summary: a human looks at the walkthrough, does the first step, looks at the next step, does it. The Gold Box pilot spent two days pre-scripting a plan before playing and reached chapter two; this replaces that.

## 1c. Two testing lanes: coverage in cheat mode, fights on their own
Winning fights with a scripted player is the hard, slow part and it is not what a release test needs. So the full playthrough runs as **two lanes**. The **coverage lane** plays the whole walkthrough, title screen to finale, with a debug-only switch that resolves every fight as an instant win (never present in the player build, asserted by a test); it sweeps every map, dialogue, item and scripted event at machine speed and every non-combat failure is a finding; levelling comes from the content completed with the fights counted as won. The **combat lane** tests every scripted fight on its own in the fight harness: the party at the level the guides say for that point, many seeds, win rate and casualties against the qualifying bar, a port audit when the numbers look wrong. A human tester does the same with a cheat code and a save file (owner ruling, Gold Box pilot 2026-10-25, after two days lost to the fighter).

## 1a. Two experts above the specialists
- A **Strategy Expert**: one persistent agent with long memory that accumulates how to beat games, within a title and across the genre, from manuals, journals and published guides and from our own play (every finding, every fight ledger). It keeps a cross-game strategy file in the method case and a per-title knowledge file; it writes no plan steps, it answers questions and reviews plans; after every chapter and every title it writes down what it learned.
- A **Player Expert**: consults the Strategy Expert before each chapter plan and at every blocker or finding, and directs the domain specialists (navigation, combat, character management, dialogue), who do not plan without its brief.
- The executor stays model-free. However many levels the game needs may sit between the two experts and the specialists. The owner's summary of the failure this corrects: a plan without strategy only tests mechanics (the Gold Box pilot spent a day proving that Haste ages a character).

## 2. Specialist planners, adapted to the game
The plan is written by **specialist lanes, one per game domain**, each reading its own slice of the decode and writing its own section of one shared plan schema (`docs/PLAYTHROUGH_SCHEMA.md` in the case repo: N sections + shared checkpoints). The executor has one policy module per domain; a blocker is routed to the specialist that owns the failing step. The domains are **derived from the game's systems at kickoff**, not fixed:
- Gold Box (party RPG): navigation (maps, exits, triggers, answers, save points), combat (per-encounter fight plan, spells, targets, retreat), party management (creation, training, equipment, rest, memorisation).
- An open-world RPG such as Darklands: travel, towns and encounters, real-time combat, alchemy and saints, the storyline arc.
- A space/trading game such as Star Command: navigation, ship combat, crew and economy.
- A strategy title: economy, build order, battle, campaign flow.
The split is recorded in `acceptance/<title>/play/DOMAINS.md` with the decode inputs per domain. The ledger measures blockers by domain and time-to-close per specialist, so the next title starts from what worked.

### 2a. Capability negotiation (spec note #1, Gold Box pilot 2026-10-24)
A plan declares the extension capabilities its steps use (for example: area-effect aiming with bounce safety, per-monster spell exclusions, retreat on a condition); the executor publishes the capabilities it implements. A step that needs a capability the executor lacks is a **PLAN blocker raised before the step runs**, never a silent fallback or a best-effort approximation; free-text notes never carry behaviour. New capabilities are added to the executor as policy code with tests, then the plan may use them.

### 2b. A published action vocabulary and a static plan check (spec note #2, Gold Box pilot 2026-10-24)
Beside its capability list the executor publishes the **exact action vocabulary** per domain: step kinds, fields, value types, and what each check verifies. That vocabulary is the planners' contract: a plan uses only those fields, prose lives only in a `why` field the executor never reads, and a rule the vocabulary cannot express is a request to the executor for a new kind or capability, never free text. A **static check** of the plan against the vocabulary runs before any live run, so a contract mismatch is caught at plan time and never costs a live iteration (the pilot spent its first two blockers on exactly this).

### 2c. Cross-domain requests as plan data (spec note #3, Gold Box pilot 2026-10-24)
The specialist split creates hand-off seams: combat needs a protection that party must cast at a stop, party adds the stop, nav must route into it. Each unpoliced seam costs a live iteration (the pilot spent two). So a **cross-domain request is first-class plan data**, not a free-text log: it carries an id, the requesting and owning domains, and a state (open, or met by a named plan item). The static pre-run check fails on any open request and on any **orphan**: a fight or stop no route step plays, a capability or precondition no earlier item produces. A specialist's change is not done until the static check is clean across all domains. Every request carries a **scope**, the checkpoint or chapter by which it must be met (whole-game means by the finale); the static check and the live run block only on open requests scoped at or before the segment being run and report the rest as pending, and large obligations are split per chapter, so the plan converges chapter by chapter alongside the run instead of waiting on every whole-game request (spec note #10: one wave of rulings produced 62 open requests and hours of a blocked run).

## 3. The state the plan plays with
A **typical player state progressed only by play**: for a party RPG, a typical party built by the game's own creation rules that levels only by playing; for other genres, the equivalent (a starting ship, a starting economy). No boosted test states, no pre-levelled saves. A normally progressed state that cannot pass a mandatory obstacle is a **finding** to investigate as a suspected port bug first (the Treasures and Dark Queen precedents), never a reason to boost. The typical state is **played by a good player**, not a naive one: every buff the game allows before a fight, every upgrade the moment it is available (training, gear, found items). A mandatory obstacle lost with full buffs and upgrades is the finding; one lost without them is a plan bug (owner ruling, Gold Box pilot 2026-10-24). And the state is **as strong as reasonably possible** before every mandatory obstacle: every reachable experience source and side content, training at every level-up, the best gear and consumables the party can afford, found items used. That is the definition of the genre. The plan is therefore not the shortest route to the finale but the route a strong-state player takes; the speedrun is of that route. Some grinding is part of the game: raising the party's level is the main sub-goal of winning a role-playing game, obvious to any human player. When the next mandatory obstacle cannot be passed at the current level with full buffs and upgrades, the plan grinds (repeatable encounters near a safe rest, repeatable script content) to a level target set per obstacle by the progression specialist, trains, then proceeds; the executor provides the grind primitive. Grinding is **bounded** the way a human bounds it: content first, since scripted encounters pay the most per fight; then at most three fights per chapter at the spot with the best experience per fight computed from the decoded tables; a target still unmet after that is a wrong target, lowered to what content and those three deliver and revisited with the Strategy Expert (the Gold Box pilot once played 175 grind fights for one level, which no human would). The plan carries a **fight budget** taken from how a human finishes the game (the owner, the guides; the scripted fights are the floor and a human plays about three times that many in total, random encounters included: Secret of the Silver Blades has 49 scripted fights, so about 150 in a 20-hour game, the rest exploration, dialogue and party management); the route is the one such a player walks as a **completionist**: every side quest, every optional area and every scripted encounter completed by the finale, in a sensible order, nothing skipped; optional content above the current strength is deferred and returned to, never dropped. That is also the test's coverage: a full pass requires every scripted block entered. Fights per chapter are reported against the budget. The retry loop is a human player's: **three attempts** at a mandatory obstacle from the checkpoint with full buffs and upgrades; if all three fail, level up, then three more; repeat. Blind retry counts in the dozens are not the method. An **apparently impossible** obstacle (three clear losses, or a loss again after a grind cycle) is left alone: the plan completes **every available side quest** reachable at that point (the navigation specialist inventories the optional blocks before each mandatory obstacle; the progression specialist sets the return condition) and only then returns. Optional content is not free progress: every planned obstacle, optional or mandatory, gets a strength target, the side-content inventory is ordered by required strength, and an item above the current state is deferred to a later return rather than attempted on the way (Gold Box pilot: a side fight wiped a party that was at target for the main-line obstacle). The escalation per mandatory obstacle is therefore: three tries, grind a level and three more, all side quests and three more, then the finding, port audit first. Arriving over-prepared is itself the shortest path: the plan is optimised for first-try wins, not for the fewest decisions. The required strength for a known obstacle comes **first from the guides**: the clue book, FAQs and walkthroughs say what level a party is expected to be at the mines, the guardian or the castle, and the Strategy Expert cites that as the target; prediction and trials only confirm it or flag a disagreement for the Strategy Expert to settle. Where no guide speaks, the required strength is **predicted before the obstacle, from the decode**: the encounter's monster count, levels, hit points, attack and defence numbers and special attacks are in the decoded tables and the combat formulas are known, so the progression specialist computes a per-obstacle strength target (level, hit-point floor, protections, gear) and the route is planned to meet it ahead of time; losing three times is the fallback for a wrong estimate, not how the target is found. A target is bounded by what the game lets that state attain (level caps per class and race, progression ceilings, decoded); a shortfall beyond the ceiling is expressed in gear, consumables, protections or composition, and an obstacle that still needs more at full preparation is the finding, never a grind. The estimator is per engine, and it is calibrated empirically: each mandatory obstacle may be **test-run in isolation** (the obstacle alone on the virtual clock, synthetic states at a ladder of levels and gear, fully prepared, several seeds each) to find the lowest level that wins reliably on the first try; that level becomes the obstacle's target. Synthetic states are allowed only in these isolated trials, which are an instrument; the playthrough itself earns the target level by play. Tactics are always the best available, in every obstacle, and they are assumed best when the target is computed; they never substitute for level. So a loss at target level with best tactics is the finding, and a loss below target is a progression plan bug, never a tactics bug: no iterations are spent tuning tactics for an under-levelled state (owner rulings, Gold Box pilot 2026-10-24).

## 4. Sources for the plan
The decoded scripts and data are the ground truth. **The game's own manual, rule book and adventurer's journal are read first**, before any plan is written: reading the manual is the first thing a human player does, and it carries the rules a plan must respect (the Gold Box pilot lost a character to old age after casting Haste before 72 fights in a row, which the rule book states ages the target a year per cast). Every planner cites the manual for the rules it relies on; a mechanic the manual documents is never discovered by losing to it. Before any plan step is written, a knowledge lane produces `acceptance/<title>/play/KNOWLEDGE.md`: how a strong player beats this game, from the manual and journal and from two or three published walkthroughs, FAQs and strategy guides (consulted, cited, never copied; credit in `docs/CREDITS.md`): party build, spell and buff priorities, every trap the rules hide (aging spells, level drain, resting and encumbrance rules), per-chapter strategy, the known hard fights and their known tactics, where players grind. Every specialist reads it and cites it; the executor carries a sanity module built from its rules (an age budget per member, for one). This is a gate, not advice: the static check refuses any plan for a title whose knowledge file is missing, has no cited walkthrough and FAQ sources, or has no per-chapter strategy. The standard is **better than a human**: human strategy combined with the exact decoded numbers, never a guess a guide would have corrected. The owner's own clue books join as BYOC extras.

## 5. Models
Measured, not assumed: planners on the cheapest capable lane first (Astra), handing a step class to a stronger model when it fails repeatedly; triage and fixes on Opus; disassembly on Fable only. The pilot title's `REPORT.md` records iterations to pass, blockers by class and domain, and which model wrote the failing step and the fix that held; the roll-out uses the winning mix.

## 6. Order of adoption
Pilot on a **middle** title of the engine (not the simplest, not the hardest), refine planner, executor, schema and report there, then roll the kit to every other title as the release gate. Gold Box pilot: Secret of the Silver Blades.
